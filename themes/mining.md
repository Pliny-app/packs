# Mining

## Underground
- **shaft** — a vertical passage sunk from the surface.
- **adit** — a horizontal entrance driven into a hillside.
- **tunnel** — the everyday word for any passage underground.
- **drift** — a horizontal passage that follows the vein.
- **crosscut** — a passage driven across the vein to reach it.
- **level** — one horizontal floor of a mine, named by its depth.
- **winze** — a shaft sunk downward from inside the mine.
- **raise** — a shaft dug upward from below.
- **stope** — the hollow left where ore has been taken out.
- **face** — the working wall at the end of a passage, where the digging happens.
- **gallery** — a long passage or chamber.
- **cavern / grotto** — a large natural cave; a small, often pretty one.
- **headframe** — the tall frame over a shaft that carries the hoist; **pithead** — the whole surface area around it.
- **quarry** — an open pit cut for stone.
- **open pit** — a mine dug down from the surface in widening steps.

## Where the riches are
- **vein** — a crack in rock filled with mineral.
- **lode** — a rich vein, or a group of them; the **mother lode** is the main source.
- **seam** — a flat layer, especially of coal.
- **deposit** — any useful concentration of mineral.
- **placer** — loose gold or gems in stream sand and gravel.
- **pocket** — a small, rich patch.
- **vug** — a small hollow in rock, often lined with crystals.
- **geode** — a round stone, plain outside and hollow inside, with crystals lining the hollow.
- **outcrop** — rock that breaks the surface, where a deposit shows itself.
- **paydirt** — ground rich enough to pay for the work.
- **bonanza** — an exceptionally rich find; its opposite is **borrasca**, a barren one.

## What comes out with it
- **gangue** — the worthless mineral around the ore.
- **country rock** — the ordinary rock a vein runs through.
- **overburden** — the soil and rock removed to reach a deposit.
- **spoil / waste rock** — rock dug out and thrown aside; heaped up, it makes a **spoil heap**.
- **tailings** — the fine waste left after ore is crushed and processed.
- **slag** — the glassy waste skimmed off during smelting.

## Rock, earth and grit
### By size
- **grain-size scale** — geologists' ladder from finest to coarsest: **clay · silt · sand · granule · pebble · cobble · boulder**. On the Wentworth scale, sand runs from 1/16 to 2 mm, granules 2–4 mm, pebbles 4–64 mm, cobbles 64–256 mm, and boulders anything larger.
- **clast** — the geologist's word for any fragment of rock; *clastic* rock is made of them.

### Solid
- **rock** — the material, or a large mass of it.
- **stone** — rock as a material for building or carving, or a single small piece. (British English says *stone* for a pebble you throw; American English often says *rock*.)
- **slab · block · monolith** — a flat, thick piece; a squared piece; a single huge standing stone.
- **crag · ledge · cliff · bluff** — a rough, jutting rock; a narrow shelf; a sheer face; a steep, rounded bank.
- **tor** — a rocky outcrop on a hilltop, weathered into stacked blocks.

### Pieces
- **chunk · lump · hunk** — a thick, shapeless piece.
- **clod** — a lump of earth or clay.
- **chip · flake · sliver · splinter · shard** — small to thin, sharp pieces; *shard* also for glass and pottery.
- **fragment · debris · rubble** — a broken piece; scattered remains; broken rock and masonry.
- **pebble · cobble · boulder** — fist-sized and smaller; larger than a fist; too big to lift.
- **scree · talus** — loose, angular stones heaped on a slope or at the foot of a cliff.
- **shingle** — rounded beach pebbles.
- **nugget** — a rounded lump of native metal, usually gold.

### Loose and fine
- **dust · powder** — fine enough to float; fine enough to pour.
- **rock flour** — rock ground to powder under a glacier; it turns meltwater milky blue.
- **grit · sand · gravel** — hard, sharp grains; loose grains up to 2 mm; small stones.
- **granules · granulate** — small grains; *granulate* is an industrial term, rare in everyday English.
- **aggregate** — crushed stone, gravel and sand used for building.
- **silt** — grains finer than sand, smooth to the touch.
- **clay** — the finest particles; sticky when wet, hard when dried or fired.
- **loam** — a balanced mix of sand, silt and clay; good, crumbly soil.
- **marl** — clay rich in lime.
- **loess** — fine, yellowish silt laid down by wind.
- **soil · earth · dirt · ground** — the everyday words; *dirt* is mostly American for loose earth.
- **humus** — dark, decayed plant matter in soil.
- **peat** — waterlogged, half-decayed plants, cut and dried as fuel.

### Wet
- **mud · muck · mire** — wet earth; dirty, sticky mud; deep mud you sink in.
- **sludge · slurry** — thick, wet waste; a pourable mix of solids and water, as pumped from mines.
- **ooze** — soft mud on a lake or sea floor.
- **quicksand** — sand so saturated it can't bear weight.

### Texture
- **loose · compacted · packed** — falling apart; pressed tight; pressed hard by weight or tramping.
- **friable** — crumbling easily in the hand.
- **cohesive** — sticking together, like clay.
- **coarse · fine · powdery · granular** — by grain.
- **rounded · angular** — worn smooth by water; sharp-edged and freshly broken.
- **well-sorted · poorly sorted** — grains all one size; a jumble of sizes.

## Layers of the ground
### From the surface down
- **leaf litter · humus** — dead leaves and plant matter on the surface; the dark, rotted layer just beneath.
- **topsoil** — the dark, fertile top layer, full of roots and life.
- **subsoil** — paler, denser soil below, holding clay and minerals washed down from above.
- **soil horizons** — soil scientists' layers: **O** (organic litter), **A** (topsoil), **E** (pale, leached layer), **B** (subsoil), **C** (broken-up parent rock), **R** (bedrock).
- **hardpan** — a cemented layer that roots and water can't get through; in dry regions, **caliche**.
- **regolith** — all the loose material above bedrock: soil, sand, broken rock.
- **saprolite** — rock rotted in place, soft enough to dig but still showing its original grain.
- **bedrock** — the solid rock beneath all loose material.
- **water table** — the depth below which the ground is saturated with water.
- **aquifer** — a layer of rock, sand or gravel that holds and passes groundwater.
- **permafrost** — ground frozen year-round.

### Rock layers
- **strata** — rock laid down in layers (singular: *stratum*).
- **bed · bedding plane** — a single layer of sedimentary rock; the surface between two beds.
- **seam · lens** — a thin layer of something valuable, such as coal; a layer that thins out to nothing at the edges.
- **sill · dike** (UK *dyke*) — a sheet of once-molten rock forced between layers; one cutting across them.
- **fault** — a break where rock on one side has slipped past the other.
- **fold · anticline · syncline** — layers bent; bent upward into an arch; bent down into a trough.
- **unconformity** — a gap in the layers where rock wore away before new layers formed; missing time.
- **superposition** — the rule that lower layers are older than those above.
- **marker bed** — a distinctive layer, such as volcanic ash, used to match strata from place to place.
- **fossil bed** — a layer rich in fossils.
- **stratigraphy** — the study of rock layers and their order.

### The deep Earth
- **crust** — the thin rocky skin; roughly 5–10 km thick under oceans, 30–50 km under continents.
- **Moho** — the boundary between crust and mantle, after the Croatian seismologist Andrija Mohorovičić. The deepest borehole ever drilled, the Kola Superdeep in Russia, reached about 12 km without getting near it.
- **mantle** — hot, solid rock that flows very slowly over millions of years.
- **lithosphere · asthenosphere** — the rigid outer shell, broken into tectonic plates; the softer layer of mantle it rides on.
- **outer core · inner core** — liquid iron and nickel, whose motion makes Earth's magnetic field; a solid iron-nickel ball at the center.
- **geothermal gradient** — the ground gets hotter with depth, on average about 25 °C per kilometre; deep mines need cooling.
- **magma · lava** — molten rock underground; the same rock once it reaches the surface.

## Rocks
- **igneous · sedimentary · metamorphic** — rock formed from cooled magma; from settled layers; from older rock changed by heat and pressure.
- **granite** — hard, speckled igneous rock with visible crystals.
- **basalt** — dark, fine volcanic rock that sometimes cools into columns.
- **obsidian** — black volcanic glass that breaks to razor edges.
- **pumice** — frothy volcanic rock, light enough to float.
- **tuff** — rock made of compacted volcanic ash.
- **limestone** — pale rock built from shells and skeletons; it fizzes in acid.
- **chalk** — soft, white, crumbly limestone.
- **sandstone** — sand grains pressed into rock, often warm in color.
- **shale** — mud turned to stone that splits into thin layers.
- **slate** — shale hardened further, splitting into flat sheets for roofs.
- **marble** — limestone recrystallized by heat; smooth and often veined.
- **quartzite** — sandstone fused into a very hard rock.
- **gneiss** — banded metamorphic rock in light and dark stripes. Pronounced "nice."
- **schist** — flaky rock that glitters with mica.
- **flint / chert** — hard, glassy stone that sparks against steel.
- **conglomerate** — rounded pebbles cemented together.
- **breccia** — sharp, angular fragments cemented together.
- **soapstone** — soft, smooth stone that feels greasy and carves easily.

## Common minerals
- **quartz** — clear or milky crystal, one of the most common minerals in the Earth's crust.
- **feldspar** — the most common mineral group in the crust; pink, white or grey.
- **mica** — splits into thin, shiny, flexible sheets.
- **calcite** — soft and common; clear calcite doubles whatever you see through it.
- **gypsum** — soft enough to scratch with a fingernail.
- **talc** — the softest mineral; soapy to the touch.
- **clay** — fine, sticky earth that hardens when fired.

## Ores
- **ore** — rock with enough metal in it to be worth mining.
- **hematite** — iron ore, grey to red-black, leaving a red-brown streak.
- **magnetite** — black iron ore, naturally magnetic. A magnetized piece is a **lodestone**.
- **pyrite** — brassy cubes known as **fool's gold**.
- **chalcopyrite** — brassy-yellow copper ore.
- **bornite** — copper ore that tarnishes into blues and purples; also called **peacock ore**.
- **malachite** — copper ore with green bands, often in rounded, grape-like masses.
- **azurite** — deep blue copper ore, often found with malachite.
- **galena** — heavy, silvery lead ore that breaks into cubes.
- **cassiterite** — dark, heavy tin ore.
- **sphalerite** — zinc ore, resinous and brown to black.
- **bauxite** — reddish, earthy rock that is the main ore of aluminum.
- **cinnabar** — bright red mercury ore, beautiful and toxic.
- **stibnite** — antimony ore in long, silvery needles.
- **wolframite** — dark, heavy tungsten ore.
- **molybdenite** — soft and greasy, easily mistaken for graphite.
- **native metal** — metal found pure, not locked in a mineral: native copper, silver, gold.
- **nugget · flake · dust** — gold found as a lump, a thin scrap, or fine grains.
- **coal** — combustible rock. **Lignite** is soft and brown, **bituminous** is the common black, and **anthracite** is hard and glossy and burns the cleanest.

## Metals
- **iron** — strong and common; it rusts.
- **steel** — iron with a little carbon, harder and tougher.
- **pig iron** — crude iron straight from the furnace.
- **copper** — warm orange-red; it greens with age.
- **tin** — soft and silvery.
- **bronze** — copper and tin.
- **brass** — copper and zinc, golden-yellow.
- **pewter** — a soft grey alloy, mostly tin.
- **lead** — heavy, soft and dull grey.
- **zinc** — bluish-grey, used to protect steel.
- **silver** — bright white; it tarnishes black.
- **gold** — soft, heavy and never tarnishes.
- **electrum** — a pale, natural blend of gold and silver.
- **platinum** — dense, silver-white and rarer than gold.
- **meteoric iron** — iron-nickel from space, with crosshatched crystal patterns when etched.
- **orichalcum** — the lost metal of Plato's Atlantis; in ancient texts, also a brass-like alloy.
- **adamant** — the legendary unbreakable substance of old myth and poetry.

## Gems and crystals
- **diamond** — pure carbon, the hardest natural mineral; famous for its fire.
- **corundum** — the mineral of **ruby** (red) and **sapphire** (blue, and other colors).
- **beryl** — the mineral of **emerald** (green), **aquamarine** (sea-blue) and **morganite** (pink).
- **quartz gems** — **amethyst** (purple), **citrine** (yellow), **rose quartz** (pink), **smoky quartz** (brown-grey), **rock crystal** (clear).
- **chalcedony** — fine-grained quartz in many forms: **agate** (banded), **onyx** (black-and-white bands), **carnelian** (orange-red), **jasper** (opaque, often red), **bloodstone** (green with red spots).
- **topaz** — hard and clear; golden, blue or colorless.
- **garnet** — usually deep red, though it comes in nearly every color.
- **peridot** — olive-green, the gem form of olivine.
- **spinel** — red spinel has long been mistaken for ruby.
- **tourmaline** — every color; *watermelon tourmaline* is pink at the heart and green at the rim.
- **zircon** — brilliant and old; some grains are among the oldest minerals on Earth.
- **opal** — flashes patches of rainbow color as it moves.
- **turquoise** — sky-blue to green, often webbed with dark veins.
- **lapis lazuli** — deep blue rock flecked with gold-colored pyrite.
- **jade** — tough green stone; two minerals, **nephrite** and **jadeite**, share the name.
- **moonstone** — a soft blue-white glow that floats across the surface.
- **labradorite** — grey until tilted, then it flashes blue and gold.
- **tiger's eye** — golden-brown, with a band of light that slides along it.
- **fluorite** — purple, green or blue; it often glows under ultraviolet light, and gave *fluorescence* its name.
- **selenite** — clear, glassy gypsum, often in long blades.
- **celestite** — pale sky-blue crystals.
- **rhodochrosite** — rose-pink with wavy bands.
- **amber** — fossilized tree resin, warm and light, sometimes holding an insect.
- **jet** — fossilized wood, black and light enough to polish into jewelry.

## Describing stone
- **luster** (UK *lustre*) — how a surface reflects light. It can be *metallic*, *vitreous* (glassy), *adamantine* (diamond-bright), *pearly*, *silky*, *greasy*, *waxy*, *resinous* or *dull/earthy*.
- **transparent · translucent · opaque** — see-through; light passes but blurred; no light passes.
- **streak** — the color of a mineral's powder, scraped on rough porcelain; often not its surface color.
- **hardness** — resistance to scratching. The **Mohs scale** runs from 1 (talc) through 7 (quartz) to 10 (diamond).
- **cleavage** — splitting cleanly along flat planes.
- **fracture** — breaking unevenly. **Conchoidal** fracture leaves smooth, shell-like curves, as in glass and flint.
- **crystal habit** — the shape a mineral grows in:
  - *cubic* — boxy
  - *prismatic* — long and many-sided
  - *acicular* — needle-thin
  - *botryoidal* — in rounded bunches, like grapes
  - *massive* — no visible crystals
- **druzy** — a sparkling crust of tiny crystals.
- **cluster** — many crystals grown together from one base.
- **matrix** — the host rock a crystal or gem is still sitting in.
- **specimen** — a piece kept for display rather than processing.
- **nodule** — a rounded lump that formed inside other rock.
- **banding** — stripes of color laid down in layers.
- **inclusion** — something trapped inside a crystal: a bubble, a needle, another mineral.
- **fire** — flashes of rainbow color thrown back by a cut gem.
- **play-of-color** — opal's shifting patches of color.
- **chatoyancy** — a "cat's eye": a band of light gliding across a polished stone.
- **asterism** — a star of light on a polished stone, as in a star sapphire.
- **adularescence** — the floating glow of moonstone.
- **labradorescence** — the sudden metallic flash of labradorite.
- **pleochroism** — showing different colors from different angles.
- **fluorescence** — glowing under ultraviolet light.

## Grading and value
- **rough** — uncut, as it came from the ground.
- **raw · crude · refined · pure** — the stages from rock to metal.
- **trace · speck · grain · chip · chunk · lump · slab · boulder** — sizes, from smallest to largest.
- **flawless · clear · included · cloudy · cracked · chipped** — gem quality, from best to worst.
- **carat** — gem weight; one carat is 0.2 grams. (Gold purity is measured in **karats**, 24 being pure.)
- **cut** — the shape a gem is given. A **brilliant** is round and many-faceted, a **cabochon** is a smooth dome with no facets, and a **baguette** is a slim rectangle.
- **facet** — one flat, polished face of a cut gem.
- **precious · semi-precious** — the traditional split: the first is diamond, ruby, sapphire and emerald; the second, everything else.
- **assay** — a test of how much metal an ore holds.
- **ingot · bar** — metal cast into a block for storing or selling.

## Tools
- **pickaxe / pick** — the miner's emblem: a pointed head on a long handle.
- **mattock** — a pick with one blade flattened for loosening soil and roots.
- **rock hammer** — a geologist's small hammer: one flat end, one pick or chisel end.
- **sledgehammer** — a heavy two-handed hammer.
- **chisel · gad · wedge** — a cutting edge; a pointed steel wedge for splitting rock; any wedge driven into a crack.
- **pry bar / crowbar** — for levering rock loose.
- **shovel · spade** — the first scoops; the second cuts.
- **drill** — a **hand steel** struck with a hammer; a **jackhammer** or **rock drill** driven by air.
- **auger** — a spiral drill for boring holes.
- **blasting powder · dynamite · fuse · detonator** — what clears rock when picks won't.
- **headlamp** — a light worn on the helmet.
- **carbide lamp** — an old lamp burning gas from water dripping on carbide.
- **safety lamp** — a flame lamp guarded by mesh so it won't ignite gas; the **Davy lamp** is the classic.
- **hard hat** — a helmet against falling rock.
- **minecart** — a wheeled cart on rails; called a **tub** or **hutch** in British mines.
- **windlass · winch · hoist** — a hand crank; a drum and cable; any machine that lifts.
- **cage** — the lift that carries miners up and down the shaft.
- **kibble** — a large bucket raised and lowered in a shaft.
- **conveyor** — a moving belt that carries ore away.
- **gold pan** — a shallow pan for swirling gravel away from heavy gold.
- **sluice** — a long wooden trough where running water washes gravel and leaves gold behind.
- **rocker / cradle** — a box rocked by hand to sort gold from gravel.
- **riddle · sieve · trommel** — a coarse sieve; a fine one; a big rotating drum sieve.
- **stamp mill** — heavy hammers that pound ore to sand.
- **crusher** — any machine that breaks rock.
- **furnace · smelter · crucible · bellows** — the heat, the works, the pot, the breath.
- **forge · anvil** — where metal is heated and hammered into shape.
- **loupe** — a small magnifier for looking into stones.
- **tumbler** — a turning drum that polishes rough stones over days.
- **pit props** — timber posts that hold up the roof.

## The work
- **dig · mine · excavate** — the general words.
- **hew** — to cut rock or coal by hand.
- **quarry** — to cut stone from an open pit.
- **delve** — to dig deep; also to search.
- **tunnel · bore · drill** — to make a passage, or a hole.
- **chip · chisel · hack · prise** — small, careful work, or rough, forceful work.
- **blast** — to break rock with explosives.
- **prospect** — to search for a deposit.
- **strike** — to find a vein or deposit; to **strike it rich** is to find a rich one.
- **pan · sift · sluice · wash** — to separate gold or gems from gravel.
- **sort · grade** — to separate by kind or quality.
- **haul · hoist · cart** — to move what's been dug.
- **smelt · refine · cast** — to melt metal out of ore; to purify it; to pour it into molds.
- **cut · facet · polish · tumble** — to shape and finish a stone.
- **shore up** — to brace with props.
- **peter out** — of a vein, to dwindle to nothing; the idiom is often traced to mining.
- **show color** — a panner's phrase: traces of gold appearing in the pan.

## Sound, touch and light
- **clink · chink · tink** — small, bright strikes of metal on stone.
- **clatter · rattle** — loose rock tumbling or a cart on rails.
- **crunch · crumble · grind** — rock giving way under weight or turning to grit.
- **rumble · thud · boom** — distant falls and blasts.
- **drip · echo** — the quiet of a mine.
- **gritty · chalky · crumbly · glassy · waxy · smooth · cool · damp** — how stone feels.
- **glint · gleam · glitter · sparkle · glow** — how ore and gems catch light.

## Hazards
- **cave-in · rockfall** — the roof or walls collapsing.
- **rockburst** — rock bursting violently from a stressed wall.
- **flooding** — water breaking in; pumps fought it constantly.
- **firedamp** — methane; explosive.
- **blackdamp** — air with too little oxygen to breathe; also called **chokedamp**.
- **afterdamp** — poisonous gas left after an explosion.
- **canary** — kept in coal mines because it sickens from bad air before people do.

## Lore and history
- **tommyknockers / knockers** — Cornish mine spirits whose tapping warned of collapse, or led to ore. Cornish miners carried the belief to America.
- **kobold** — a German mine goblin. Miners blamed kobolds for ores that gave off poisonous fumes and no useful metal, and **cobalt** is named after it.
- **nickel** — from German *Kupfernickel*, roughly "copper demon": an ore that looked like copper but wouldn't yield any.
- **Saint Barbara** — patron saint of miners.
- **Glück auf** — the traditional German miners' greeting, a wish for a safe return to the surface.
- **pit pony** — a pony that hauled carts underground.
- **dowsing rod** — a forked stick said, in folklore, to twitch over water or ore.
