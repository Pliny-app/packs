# English Pitfalls for Italian Speakers

Each entry gives the slip, the Italian behind it, and what a native speaker would write instead.

## False friends
- **eventually** — from *eventualmente*, "if needed, possibly". In English it means "in the end".
- **actual · actually** — from *attuale · attualmente*, "current · currently". In English they mean "real · in fact".
- **anticipate** — from *anticipare*, "bring forward". "Can we anticipate the meeting?" → *bring the meeting forward*. In English, *anticipate* means expect or prepare for.
- **in fact** — from *infatti*, "indeed, sure enough". "I said it would rain, and in fact it rained" → *and sure enough, it did*. In English, *in fact* usually adds something stronger or contrasting: "It's not cheap; in fact, it's the most expensive."
- **argument** — *argomento* is a topic. "The next argument" → *the next topic, the next item*.
- **consistent** — *consistente* means substantial. "A consistent increase" → *a significant increase*. In English, *consistent* means steady.
- **pretend** — from *pretendere*, "expect, demand". "I pretend an answer" → *I expect an answer*. In English, *make believe*.
- **delude · deluded** — from *deludere · deluso*, "disappoint · disappointed". In English, *deluded* means fooled.
- **firm** — from *firmare*, "sign". "Please firm the contract" → *sign the contract*.
- **assist** — from *assistere*, "attend". "I assisted to the presentation" → *I attended*.
- **attend** — from *attendere*, "wait". Write *wait for, look forward to*.
- **control** — from *controllare*, "check". Write *check, verify*.
- **realize** — from *realizzare*, "make, build, carry out". "We realized the website" → *we built the website*.
- **formation** — *formazione* is training. Write *training*.
- **confront** — from *confrontare*, "compare". "Let's confront the two offers" → *compare*.
- **it occurs** — from *occorre*, "it is necessary". "It occurs to send the file" → *we need to send the file*. In English, *it occurs to me* means I've realized.
- **it's not the case** — from *non è il caso*, "there's no need, it's not appropriate". "It's not the case to name names" → *there's no need to name names*. In English, *it's not the case* means it's not true.
- **convenient** — *conveniente* means good value. "A convenient price" → *a good price*. In English, *convenient* means handy.
- **educated** — *educato* means polite. Write *polite, well-mannered*. In English, *educated* means schooled.
- **sensible** — from *sensibile*, "sensitive". In English it means reasonable.
- **annoyed** — *annoiato* means bored. In English, *annoyed* means irritated.
- **sympathetic** — from *simpatico*, "nice, likeable". In English it means compassionate.
- **notice** — *notizia* is news. Write *news*.
- **fabric** — *fabbrica* is a factory.
- **library** — *libreria* is a bookshop. A *biblioteca* is a library.
- **agenda** — *agenda* is a diary or planner. In English, the list of topics for a meeting.
- **stage** — an internship. Write *internship*.
- **responsible** (as a noun) — *il responsabile* is the person in charge. "The responsible of sales" → *the head of sales*.

## Words that don't exist
- **reasonment** — from *ragionamento*. Write *reasoning*.
- **differents · importants · interestings** — English adjectives never take a plural *-s*. Write *different options, important points*.

## One Italian word, two English ones
- **fare → make or do** — you *make* a mistake, a decision, a call; you *do* homework, a favor, business. "I did a mistake" → *I made a mistake*. Cooking a dish is *making* it.
- **vedere → see or watch** — you *see* what crosses your eyes, you *watch* something on purpose: *watch TV*, *watch the match*. (*See a film* is fine.)
- **potere → can or may** — both work in questions. *May I…?* is more polite, *Can I…?* more casual.
- **torta → cake, pie or tart** — *cake* is sponge, a *pie* has a pastry lid, a *tart* is open-topped.
- **perdere → lose or waste** — you *lose* things, you *waste* time. "Let's not lose time" → *let's not waste time*.

## Translated phrases
- **you have reason** — from *hai ragione*. Write *you're right*.
- **nothing** (as a reply to thanks) — from *di niente*. Write *you're welcome, no problem, don't mention it*.
- **good work · good job** (as a greeting) — from *buon lavoro*. Write *have a good day*. In English both are praise for work already done.
- **make a question** — from *fare una domanda*. Write *ask a question*.
- **make a photo · make a pause** — Write *take a photo, take a break*.
- **I have 30 years** — from *ho 30 anni*. Write *I'm 30*.
- **depends from** — from *dipende da*. Write *depends on*.
- **married with** — from *sposato con*. Write *married to*.

## Grammar slips
- **since three years** — from *da tre anni*. Write *for three years*, with the present perfect: *I've lived here for nine years*.
- **what did you did?** — the past goes on *did* only. Write *What did you do? · What did I tell you?*
- **I didn't do nothing** — from *non ho fatto niente*. English uses one negative. Write *I didn't do anything*, or *I did nothing*.
- **if I was you** — from *se fossi te*. Common in speech, but formal writing keeps *if I were you*.
- **the boys jumps** — the *-s* goes on the verb only for one subject: *the boy jumps, the boys jump*.
- **is raining · is important to…** — Italian drops the subject because the verb shows it. English can't: *it's raining, it's important to…*
- **my bike, she is fast** — English objects are *it*, not he or she. (Ships and countries are old exceptions.)
- **explain me · say me · discuss about / of** — Write *explain to me · tell me · discuss*.
- **informations · advices · feedbacks · equipments** — uncountable in English: *information, advice, feedback, equipment*.
- **I'm agree** — from *sono d'accordo*. Write *I agree*.

## Prepositions
- **come in Italy · arrive to Milan** — from *in Italia, a Milano*. English moves *to* a place and arrives *in* a city or country, or *at* a building: *come to Italy, arrive in Milan, arrive at the office*.
- **with the train · by foot** — Write *by train, by car*, but *on foot*.

## Articles
Italian uses *the* where English leaves it out.
- **general and abstract ideas** — "The life is beautiful", "The patience is a virtue" → *Life is beautiful, Patience is a virtue*.
- **materials and things in general** — "The steel is expensive", "The cats are curious" → *Steel is expensive, Cats are curious* (meaning all of them).
- **places used for their purpose** — "go to the school, go to the bed, be at the home" → *go to school, go to bed, be at home*. (*The school* means a particular building: "I'm meeting him at the school.")
- **set phrases** — *by train, at dawn, at an early age, in bed* take no article.

## Word order and style
- **adjective after the noun** — "a car red, a solution elegant" → *a red car, an elegant solution*. English adjectives come first.
- **the car of the mother of my girlfriend** — Italian has no possessive *'s*, so chains of *of* pile up. Write *my girlfriend's mother's car*.
- **formal Latin words where plain ones fit** — Italian speakers reach for the English word that looks like the Italian one. It's correct but stiff: *commence → start*, *terminate → end*, *renounce → give up*, *response → reply*, *error → mistake*, *prohibit → ban*, *arrive → get to*, *sufficient → enough*.
- **long sentences** — Italian prose values long, layered sentences with many subordinate clauses. English readers prefer them shorter. Split at *which*, *although* and *since*.
- **British or American** — most Italians learn British English. Pick one spelling (*colour* or *color*) and keep to it.
- **quite · quiet** — easy to swap in writing: *quite* means fairly, *quiet* means silent.

## Interjections
Translated word for word, these come out strange. What English says instead:
- **Boh! · Mah!** — *No idea. · Who knows?*
- **Caspita!** — *Wow! · Gosh!*
- **Oddio! · Mamma mia!** — *Oh my God! · Good grief!*
- **Manco per idea! · Manco per sogno!** — *No way! · Not a chance!*
- **Per niente!** — *Not at all!* (not "for nothing")
- **Per carità!** — *God forbid! · Please, no!*
- **Che peccato! · Peccato!** — *What a shame! · Too bad.*
- **Che bello!** — *How lovely! · That's great!* (not "what beautiful"). Of a person, *Che bello!* is *How handsome!* and *Che bella!* is *How beautiful!*
- **Che buono!** — *Delicious!*
- **Che barba!** — *How boring!* (not "what a beard")
- **Uffa!** — *Ugh!*
- **Evviva!** — *Hooray!*
- **Ma dai!** — *Come on! · No way!*
- **Non mi dire!** — *You don't say!*
- **Come no!** — *Of course! · Sure!* (not "how not")
- **Senz'altro!** — *Definitely!*
- **Vabbè** — *Oh well · Fine.*
- **Ecco** — *There you go · Here it is.*
- **Magari!** — *I wish! · If only!* As an adverb, *magari* can mean *maybe*, which is why it gets mixed up.
- **Ohimè!** — *Oh dear!*
- **Coraggio!** — *Chin up! · Come on, you can do it.*
- **In bocca al lupo!** — *Good luck! · Break a leg!*

## English words that aren't English
- **location** — the venue of an event, or a film set. Write *venue*.
- **open space** — an open-plan office.
- **spot** — a TV or radio ad. Write *commercial, ad*.
- **book** — a portfolio, as in a model's book. Write *portfolio*.
- **smoking** — a tuxedo. On an invitation, write *black tie*.

## Emails
- **Kind Mr Rossi · Gentle Mr Rossi** — from *Gentile Sig. Rossi*. Write *Dear Mr Rossi*.
- **Dr · Eng.** — *Dott.* is used for any graduate and *Ing.* for engineers. In English, *Dr* means a doctorate or a physician. Use the name alone.
- **in attachment · in attached** — from *in allegato*. Write *attached is, please find attached*.
- **waiting for your kind reply** — from *in attesa di un suo cortese riscontro*. Write *I look forward to hearing from you*.
- **distinct greetings · cordial greetings** — from *distinti saluti · cordiali saluti*. Write *Kind regards · Best regards*.

## Numbers, dates and time
- **billion** — Italian *miliardo* is the English *billion*; the rare Italian *bilione* is the English *trillion*.
- **3,5 · 1.000** — Italian uses a decimal comma and a thousands point. English: *3.5 · 1,000*.
- **03/04/2026** — 3 April in Italy, but an American reads March 4. Write *3 April 2026*.
