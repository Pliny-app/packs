# Mangled Idioms

Idioms go wrong in two main ways:
- A **malaphor** blends two idioms into one: *we'll burn that bridge when we get to it*. The word itself blends *malapropism* and *metaphor*, and was coined by Lawrence Harrison in 1976.
- An **eggcorn** swaps a word for one that sounds the same and seems to make sense: *escape goat* for *scapegoat*. The name comes from someone writing *egg corn* for *acorn*, and was suggested by the linguist Geoffrey Pullum in 2003.


## Malaphors
Two idioms blended into one.
- **we'll burn that bridge when we get to it** — *cross that bridge when we come to it* + *burn your bridges*. Funny because it promises a disaster.
- **the cows come home to roost** — *till the cows come home* (forever) + *the chickens come home to roost* (consequences return).
- **every cloud has a silver spoon in its mouth** — *every cloud has a silver lining* + *born with a silver spoon in your mouth*.
- **it's not rocket surgery** — *it's not rocket science* + *it's not brain surgery*. Often used on purpose.
- **a walk in the cake** — *a walk in the park* + *a piece of cake*. Both mean easy.
- **not the sharpest knife in the shed** — *not the sharpest knife in the drawer* + *not the sharpest tool in the shed*.
- **a horse of a different feather** — *a horse of a different color* + *birds of a feather*.
- **that's the way the cookie bounces** — *that's the way the cookie crumbles* + *that's the way the ball bounces*.
- **a whole new ball of wax** — *a whole new ball game* + *the whole ball of wax*.

## Eggcorns
A word misheard and replaced with one that seems logical. Correct form after the arrow.
- **all intensive purposes** → *all intents and purposes*: in every practical sense.
- **biting my time** → *biding my time*: waiting patiently for the right moment.
- **growth sprout** → *growth spurt*: a sudden period of growing.
- **escape goat** → *scapegoat*: someone blamed for others' faults.
- **two peas in a pond** → *two peas in a pod*: very alike.
- **a hard road to hoe** → *a hard row to hoe*: a difficult task. You hoe rows of crops, not roads.
- **put a steak in the ground · put a stick in the ground** → *put a stake in the ground*: commit publicly to a position or target.
- **nip it in the butt** → *nip it in the bud*: stop something early.
- **on tender hooks** → *on tenterhooks*: anxious, in suspense. Tenterhooks held cloth stretched on a frame.
- **deep-seeded** → *deep-seated*: firmly established.
- **free reign** → *free rein*: complete freedom. The image is a horse's reins, not a king's reign.
- **case and point** → *case in point*: a fitting example.
- **one in the same** → *one and the same*: identical.
- **mute point** → *moot point*: no longer worth discussing.
- **should of · could of · would of** → *should have, could have, would have*. Misheard from *should've*.
- **statue of limitations** → *statute of limitations*: the legal time limit for bringing a case.
- **old wise tale** → *old wives' tale*: a superstition passed down.
- **piece of mind** → *peace of mind*. (*Give someone a piece of your mind* is a different idiom: tell them off.)
- **sneak peak** → *sneak peek*: an early look.
- **wet your appetite** → *whet your appetite*: sharpen it.
- **beckon call** → *beck and call*: always ready to serve.
- **doggy-dog world** → *dog-eat-dog world*: ruthless competition.
- **tow the line** → *toe the line*: obey the rules, as in standing with your toes at a line.
- **baited breath** → *bated breath*: holding your breath (*bated* is short for *abated*).
- **make due** → *make do*: manage with what you have.
- **pre-Madonna** → *prima donna*: a temperamental, self-important person.
- **damp squid** → *damp squib* (chiefly UK): a disappointing anticlimax. A squib is a small firework.
- **old-timer's disease** → *Alzheimer's disease*.

## Once wrong, now standard
Some mistakes won. These are the accepted forms today, or accepted alongside the original.
- **curry favor** (UK *favour*) — originally *curry favel*, to groom Fauvel, a chestnut horse that stood for deceit in a medieval French satire. By the 1500s, *favel* had become *favour*. The "eggcorn" is now the only form.
- **you've got another thing coming** — originally *another think coming*, which still exists but is now the less common form. Both mean: you're badly mistaken.
- **not bat an eyelash** — a US variant of *not bat an eyelid* (UK) and *not bat an eye*. All mean: show no reaction, not even a blink.
- **butt naked** — from *buck naked*. Widely used now, though *buck naked* is still the standard form in edited writing.
- **duct tape · duck tape** — both are used. *Duck tape* is probably the older name, after the cotton duck cloth it was made from; *duct tape* is the common form today. *Duck Tape* is also a brand name.
- **chomping at the bit** — originally *champing at the bit*; *chomping* is now more common.
- **just desserts** — originally *just deserts*, "what one deserves". *Desserts* is now widespread, but *deserts* is still preferred in careful writing.
- **hone in on** — originally *home in on*. *Hone in* is common but still criticized.

## Related kinds of slip
- **malapropism** — the wrong word that sounds like the right one, often comically grand: "a pineapple of politeness" for *pinnacle*. Named after Mrs. Malaprop in Sheridan's play *The Rivals* (1775).
- **mondegreen** — a misheard lyric or line of verse. Named by the writer Sylvia Wright in 1954, after hearing the ballad line *laid him on the green* as *Lady Mondegreen*.
- **spoonerism** — swapped initial sounds: *a well-boiled icicle* for *a well-oiled bicycle*. Named after the Reverend William Spooner of Oxford.
- **folk etymology** — reshaping an unfamiliar word into familiar parts; how many eggcorns become standard.
- **mixed metaphor** — images that clash in one sentence, such as "we'll have to tighten our belts and cast the net wider". A malaphor is the idiom-sized version.
